How these were measured: a site audit and Lighthouse on mobile, before the rebuild and after it.

Situation

An iconic factory, a store that did not match it

ExpandWrap

One of the last handsewn moccasin makers in America had a store that did not match its shoes. The catalog had no structure: a flat database built on tags, a third of it stale or duplicated, three names for one leather. That broke everything on top of it: 11,525 broken links, a slow phone experience (37 out of 100), and accessibility below standard.

Business findings: Fix

Rancourt & Co. is one of the last makers of genuine handsewn moccasins in America: a third-generation factory in Lewiston, Maine, in business since 1967, private-label maker for Ralph Lauren and Cole Haan, and the maker of Team USA’s Olympic ceremony shoes, several Games in a row. Pairs run from $288 to $738. The store did not match the shoes: 11,525 broken URLs, 615 soft 404s, mobile performance 37, accessibility 85. The agency’s root cause was category pages and product naming. In other words, the data. And the brand ranked first for “penny loafers” and “resole shoes”, which I could not break.

Business findings: Protect

The brand ranked first for “penny loafers” and “resole shoes”. The rebuild had to keep every one of those URLs and the words shoppers use to find them.

Discovery workshops

Working sessions with the owner and the five-person design and marketing department, model by model: what a product is, which leathers are real, what the cobbler service can promise. We mapped how a season actually gets made, what the factory already tracks in Backbone, and what a single product is made of, then sorted the asks into low, medium and urgent. The decision log became the spec.

Task

Move without losing the ground

ExpandWrap

Design the data model and taxonomy, build a custom Shopify theme on them, keep every ranked URL, make it accessible. Scope in three tiers.

The plan

Seven phases plus hardening, a written definition of done, and the client’s effort planned per phase. Think first, prove on a sample, then migrate.

Three delivery tiers

Every wish in one list, sorted into three tiers: launch, next, later. The site map carries the colors so nobody had to ask why.

Action

Research, then data, then screens

ExpandWrap

Research, then data, then screens, all mine: a five-item navigation, product pages built from fields, a redesigned cart, shared libraries for every material, WCAG 2.1 AA and the ranked URLs built in.

Information architecture

The navigation is built from the taxonomy, not by hand: type, gender, collection and occasion are fields, so every collection page, filter and menu entry derives from the same data. Nine top-level items became five. Shoe Care and Repairs merged into one service area, with eligibility set per model. Labels come from the user tests, in the shopper’s words rather than the factory’s.

The product page

Four questions before the scroll: what is it made of, how does it fit, where is it made, what happens when it wears out. Four blocks answer them, every line from a field. Colorways share one page; width reads “Regular (D)”.

Cart and checkout

The owner’s wishlist in his words, plus the one structural problem from my audit: a returns fork at the cart. Fixed with a subscription change and a redesigned drawer.

The product data model

Every visible problem was a data problem underneath, and the fix was unification. Before, each product carried its own typed-in leather, sole, lining and care text, so the same thing had several names. Now a product is a pattern, a colorway and an upper; variants are size and width; everything else comes from one shared library: 29 uppers, 23 soles, five linings, eight lasts, 36 colors. Select the pattern and most of the product is already filled in. Rename once, every page follows.

Accessibility and search, built in

WCAG 2.1 AA built into the theme, not bolted on. The six ranked URLs kept their slugs, everything else redirected, FAQ content where Google already asks questions.

Build and test

I built it myself: a store-bought base rebuilt template by template, the catalog exported, cleaned and reimported, integrations configured, third-party apps cut from 24 to 2, everything else native Shopify. Two testing passes, one of them a plain-language walkthrough for the owner.

User testing

I tested the new store, built to the client’s vision, with a team of user testers. I wrote the test guide and moderated six sessions: four tasks, a debrief, every misread label logged. Fourteen themes came up more than once; the ones four or more people hit were fixed before launch.

Teaching the team to run it

The libraries are edited in one place and products follow. SOPs for what recurs, demos instead of documents, one rule for the old database. The walkthrough doubles as training.

Result

Designed and built by one person, run by the brand’s team

Open websiteExpandWrap

Zero broken URLs, performance 37 to 100, accessibility 85 to 100, one name per leather, third-party apps from 24 to 2, and a store the brand’s team runs.

What shipped

Designed and built end to end: custom theme, structured catalog, a navigation shaped by testers, a cart without the fork, accessibility built in, every ranked URL kept, and a department that runs it without me.
